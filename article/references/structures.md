# Structures

Eight ways to lay out one idea. Pick **one** per image. Mixing two is the most common way a
body illustration turns into a diagram.

The parent skill's `composition-patterns.md` covers how to compose a single asset — centring,
margins, black masses, overlap. That still applies. This file is about what the picture is
*doing* for the argument.

## The eight

### Flow
Input, work, output — left to right. The figure performs the middle step, not the input or
the output. Good for: pipelines, processes, how something gets made.

### System part
Three to five objects standing for parts of a system, the figure operating exactly one of
them. Never draw the whole system. Good for: where a thing sits, what feeds what.

### Before and after
The same person twice — strained on the left, settled on the right. The contrast is carried
by **posture**, not by a divider or an arrow. Good for: what changed, what a fix bought you.

### States
Two to four small vignettes of the same person in different conditions. Each one action.
Good for: user pain, stages of getting stuck, how a role changes.

### Concept object
One ordinary object drawn oversized, the person dealing with it. This is the parent spec's
"figure and oversized object", and it is the only exaggeration the style permits — so make it
count. Good for: the one abstract noun an article keeps returning to.

### Layers
Stacked forms, the figure building or climbing them. Not a pyramid, not a formal hierarchy.
Good for: a framework, a stack, a method with levels.

### Path
A route with two or three stops, the figure mid-stride along it. Good for: a journey, an
onboarding, idea to launch.

### Panels
Two to four frames, one action each. The most text-like structure — use it only when the
idea is genuinely sequential and nothing else carries it.

## Turning an abstract claim into something drawable

Articles are full of nouns nobody can draw: *alignment*, *leverage*, *technical debt*,
*context switching*. Three steps.

**1. Replace the abstraction with a physical action.**
Getting stuck, dropping something, carrying too much, sorting, waiting, handing over, packing,
unpacking, reaching, setting down, tying together, letting go.

**2. Replace the system with one ordinary object.**
A box, a drawer, a ladder, a door, an envelope, a suitcase, a shelf, a bag, a stack of paper,
a clock, a rope, a tap, a crate. One or two per image, never a pile.

Ordinary is the constraint. This style does not draw broken machines, surreal contraptions or
absurd props — that belongs to a different style. A person struggling with a genuinely large
box says everything a strange machine would, and stays inside the spec.

**3. Give the action to the figure.**
Not standing beside the object. Holding it, lifting it, stuck under it, reaching past it,
setting it down, handing it over, waiting next to it having given up on it.

### The removal test

Delete the person from the image in your head. If the idea still reads, the person was
decoration — rewrite the prompt so the action they perform *is* the idea.

This is the single check that separates a body illustration from stock art.

## Worked translations

| Abstract | Action | Object | The picture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical debt | carrying too much | stack of boxes | mid-stride, stack up past the head, one box slipping |
| Context switching | putting down, picking up | two bags | reaching for one bag while the other is still half-lifted |
| Alignment | pulling together | one rope | two figures, one rope, both leaning the same way |
| Scope creep | packing | one suitcase | kneeling on a suitcase that will not close |
| Compounding | stacking | crates | placing one crate on a stack taller than the figure |
| Information overload | sorting | loose paper | a person mid-sort, most of the sheets still unsorted |

Note what each one is not: not a metaphor explained with a label, not a chart, not a symbol.
Just a person doing a physical thing that happens to be the argument.

## No repeats inside a set

**Every image in a set gets a fresh object and a fresh action.** Two images in one article
that both show a person carrying a stack is a set that looks like it ran out of ideas — which
it did.

Before writing image N's prompt, list the objects and actions already used in images 1..N-1
and rule them out. Same for structures: do not use `flow` three times in one article.

If two sections genuinely make the same move, that is a sign they should share one
illustration, or that one of them does not need art.

The one thing that **must** stay identical across the set is the person. Lock the five
attributes from the parent's `characters.md` — hair silhouette, skin value, top, bottom, one
accessory — and restate them verbatim in every prompt.
